\section{Data Collection}
\label{sec:collection}

\subsection{Data Sources}

We collected daily data from 1 January 2010 to 20 October 2026 for four metals
and four external signals (Table~\ref{tab:sources}). All sources are public and
free. Metal prices are \emph{continuous front-month futures} prices, which is
the benchmark that physical metal suppliers price against.

\textbf{Why these external signals.} Each one is a widely cited driver of metal
prices, and testing whether they improve forecasts is part of our project:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{US Dollar Index:} metals are priced in US dollars, so a stronger
        dollar makes them more expensive for other buyers and tends to push
        prices down.
  \item \textbf{10-year Treasury yield:} gold pays no interest, so when
        interest rates rise, holding gold becomes less attractive compared with
        bonds.
  \item \textbf{WTI crude oil:} oil feeds into mining and transport costs and
        into general inflation.
  \item \textbf{Consumer Price Index:} gold is often bought as a hedge against
        inflation, which the CPI measures.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Collection Method}

Collection is done by three Python scripts in \texttt{data\_scripts/}, run in
this order:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \texttt{fetch\_metals.py} downloads the four metal futures from Yahoo
        Finance using the \texttt{yfinance} library~\cite{a_yfinance} and saves
        one CSV file per metal.
  \item \texttt{fetch\_external.py} downloads the dollar index and oil from
        Yahoo Finance, and the 10-year yield and CPI from FRED~\cite{a_fred}.
  \item \texttt{merge\_sources.py} joins all eight series on their date into one
        table, \texttt{merged\_raw.csv}.
\end{enumerate}

Every download is retried up to three times, and each step writes a line to
\texttt{data\_scripts/logs/collection.log} with the number of rows, the date
range and the number of missing values. The log is our evidence of collection;
a copy is included in the repository as \texttt{report/evidence/collection\_log.txt}.
Raw data files are not uploaded to GitHub; anyone can recreate them by running
the three scripts. All shared settings (tickers, start date and file paths) are
kept in one file, \texttt{config.py}.

\textbf{Backup sources.} During collection, FRED repeatedly refused or timed out
the connection from our network. Instead of failing, \texttt{fetch\_external.py}
automatically switched to backup sources, and recorded the switch in the log:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the 10-year yield was taken from Yahoo Finance's \texttt{\textasciicircum TNX}
        (the CBOE 10-year Treasury yield index, quoted in percent), which tracks
        FRED's \texttt{DGS10} series closely;
  \item CPI was taken directly from the US Bureau of Labor Statistics
        API~\cite{a_bls}, series \texttt{CUSR0000SA0}. This is the same
        seasonally adjusted CPI that FRED republishes as \texttt{CPIAUCSL}. The
        free API returns at most ten years per request, so the script requests
        2010--2019 and 2020--2026 separately.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Data Quality Issues Found}

Inspecting the raw data revealed the following issues, which are handled in
Section~4:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Gaps from different calendars.} About 75 rows (1.7--1.8\%) are
        missing in each daily series because markets close on different
        holidays, so a date present in one source can be absent in another.
  \item \textbf{Mixed frequencies.} CPI is published monthly, so 95\% of the
        daily rows have no CPI value. One month is also missing in the BLS
        series itself.
  \item \textbf{An impossible price.} WTI oil futures closed at about
        $-\$37$ per barrel on 20 April 2020 (Figure~\ref{fig:raw-external}). This
        was a real event caused by an expiring contract, but a negative price
        cannot be used for percentage returns.
  \item \textbf{Extreme daily moves.} The largest one-day falls are
        $-10.8\%$ for gold, $-19.0\%$ for platinum, $-22.3\%$ for copper and
        $-31.3\%$ for silver (Figure~\ref{fig:raw-returns}). Some are genuine
        market crashes; others may come from the way a continuous futures series
        jumps from one contract to the next. They must be detected and treated
        as outliers.
\end{itemize}
